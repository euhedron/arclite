Every invocation of risk, safety, or danger language — "risky", "safer", "to be safe", "could break things" — must name its concrete failure mode: what specifically goes wrong, through what mechanism, at what cost. Risk words with no named referent are affect, not analysis: they borrow the authority of a risk assessment without performing one, and they steer real decisions — ordering, scope, caution, deferral — on performed fear rather than on a consequence anyone could examine or dispute. The tell is that the language cannot survive the question "what, concretely, goes wrong?": if the answer names a mechanism, state the mechanism instead of the mood; if it names nothing, the risk does not exist and the word must go. A named risk invites counter-analysis — how likely, how costly, mitigated how — while an unnamed one forecloses it, which is exactly why the vague form persists. Siblings: fabricated urgency invents a pressure that does not exist; mislabeled finality points risk language at the wrong referent; this rule covers risk language with no referent at all. Flag a decision justified with risk or safety vocabulary where no concrete failure mode is named — especially comparative forms ("safer", "riskier") that rank options by an unstated harm.
