Risk and irreversibility are distinct and must be reasoned about separately, never collapsed into each other. Risk is a probability — the chance an action yields a bad outcome; irreversibility (finality) is a property — the certainty that the action, whatever its outcome, cannot be undone. They vary independently: an action can be irreversible yet unlikely to go wrong, or fully reversible yet likely to. Two conflations mislead, in opposite directions. One imports a probability frame onto a property — calling a one-way action "risky" or "low-risk" when the operative fact is that it cannot be undone, dressing a finality decision as a risk assessment. The other erases the probability by over-crediting verification — treating preflight checks as proof the adverse-outcome probability is now zero, so finality becomes the *only* thing left to weigh. Verification reduces risk; it does not abolish it: checks cannot cover outcomes they do not model, and for many irreversible actions the step itself is where the real outcome first appears (a dry run validates what it can locally, not remote acceptance). A residual risk therefore survives even a clean verification and still deserves its own statement. The discipline: state the residual risk and the finality each on its own terms, and justify confirming an irreversible action on both — never let "verified" collapse the residual risk to zero, nor "irreversible" masquerade as a risk level. Flag a conflation in either direction: a finality property labeled as a risk level, or a verification treated as having made an irreversible action riskless.
